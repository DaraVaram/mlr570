Vector and matrix addition

Definition

Let \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) be vectors. Their sum is defined element-wise:

\[ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = [a_1 + b_1,\ a_2 + b_2,\ \dots,\ a_n + b_n]. \]

Similarly, for matrices \(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\):

\[ \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = [a_{ij} + b_{ij}] \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, m;\ j = 1, \dots, n. \]
Intuition

Addition combines corresponding entries. For vectors this is adding directed line segments tip-to-tail in Euclidean space: walk along \(\mathbf{a}\), then walk along \(\mathbf{b}\), and the sum is where you end up.

Basic properties.

  • Commutative: \(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}\).
  • Associative: \((\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})+\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}+(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C})\).
  • Additive identity: \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{A}\), where \(\mathbf{0}\) is the zero matrix.
  • Additive inverse: \(\mathbf{A} + (-\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}\).

Where do matrices and vectors come from?

Matrices and vectors are the standard representation for real-world data and operations. Before working through the mechanics, it is worth grounding the symbols in things you already recognise.

1 · One-dimensional data (vectors)

A real-valued vector \(\mathbf{x} = [x_1,\ldots,x_n]^T\) naturally represents ordered measurements:

  • Time series: daily temperatures, stock prices, sensor readings.
  • Text features: bag-of-words counts (non-negative integers).
  • Audio snippets: amplitude samples over time.

For instance, a five-day temperature record is \(\mathbf{t} = [36.2,\, 35.8,\, 37.1,\, 36.5,\, 35.9]^T\).

2 · Images as matrices (and tensors)

A grayscale image of height \(m\) and width \(n\) is a matrix \(\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), where \(\mathbf{I}_{ij}\) holds the pixel intensity at row \(i\), column \(j\). Intensity runs from 0 (fully black) to 255 (fully white). A \(28 \times 28\) MNIST digit is therefore a matrix \(\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{28 \times 28}\), and nothing at all is lost in that translation.

\[ \mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 128 & 255 \\ 64 & 192 & 32 \\ 255 & 16 & 80 \end{bmatrix} \]

Basic operations model familiar edits: scalar multiplication changes brightness, subtraction from 255 inverts, and convolution with a small kernel blurs or detects edges.

3 · Data tables as matrices

A dataset with \(m\) examples and \(n\) features is an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), where row \(i\) is example \(i\) and column \(j\) is feature \(j\). Labels live in a vector \(\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) for regression, or a categorical vector for classification.

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 170 & 70 \\ 165 & 60 \\ 180 & 90 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Three patients (\(m=3\)), two features (\(n=2\)). Each row is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\); columns can be standardised feature-wise.

Python 
X = np.array([[170, 70],
              [165, 60],
              [180, 90]], dtype=float)

# Column-wise standardisation
X_std = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0, ddof=1)

4 · Systems of linear equations

Many real problems reduce to solving \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\):

  • Circuits (Kirchhoff's laws): solve for node voltages and currents.
  • Structural engineering: equilibrium of forces.
  • Economics: Leontief input–output models.
  • Least squares / linear regression: \(\min_\mathbf{x} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2\).
\[ \begin{cases} 2x + y = 5 \\ -x + 3y = 1 \end{cases} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Matrix multiplication

Definition

Let \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) and \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\). The product \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{AB} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}\) is defined by

\[ c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik} b_{kj}. \]

This is defined only when the number of columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) equals the number of rows of \(\mathbf{B}\).

Intuition

Each entry of \(\mathbf{C}\) combines one row of \(\mathbf{A}\) with one column of \(\mathbf{B}\). Geometrically, matrix multiplication composes linear transformations: applying \(\mathbf{AB}\) means first apply \(\mathbf{B}\), then \(\mathbf{A}\). That right-to-left reading is the reason the order matters.

Key properties.

  • Associative: \((\mathbf{AB})\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{BC})\) when the dimensions match.
  • Distributive: \(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{AB} + \mathbf{AC}\) and \((\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{AC} + \mathbf{BC}\).
  • Generally not commutative: \(\mathbf{AB} \neq \mathbf{BA}\) in general.
  • Identity: for \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), \(\ \mathbf{I}_m\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_n = \mathbf{A}\).
On "when the dimensions match"

For \(\mathbf{AB}\) to exist, the number of columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) must equal the number of rows of \(\mathbf{B}\): if \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), then \(\mathbf{B}\) must be \(n \times p\). The identity property needs the same care — for \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), the identity that works on the left is \(\mathbf{I}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\), and the one that works on the right is \(\mathbf{I}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\). Only when \(\mathbf{A}\) is square are they the same matrix.

Solving linear systems

A linear system is a collection of linear equations in the same variables. A system of \(m\) equations in \(n\) variables is written

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, \]

where \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) is the coefficient matrix, \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) the variable vector, and \(\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) the right-hand side. Exactly three outcomes are possible: a unique solution, no solution, or infinitely many. We find out which by Gaussian elimination, reducing to row echelon form (REF) or reduced row echelon form (RREF).

Example 1 · unique solution
\[ \begin{cases} x + y = 3 \\ 2x - y = 0 \end{cases} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 & 0 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \begin{aligned} R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1 &\Rightarrow \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \end{array}\right] \\ R_2 \leftarrow -\tfrac{1}{3} R_2 &\Rightarrow \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] \\ R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - R_2 &\Rightarrow \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} \]

Solution: \(x = 1\), \(y = 2\).

Example 2 · no solution
\[ \begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ 2x + 2y = 5 \end{cases} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 5 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \]

The second row now reads \(0 = 1\), a contradiction. The system is inconsistent — no solution.

Example 3 · infinitely many solutions
\[ \begin{cases} x + 2y = 4 \\ 2x + 4y = 8 \end{cases} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 8 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right] \]

The second equation was redundant. From the first row, \(x = 4 - 2y\), so with \(y = t\) free:

\[ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 2t \\ t \end{bmatrix},\quad t \in \mathbb{R} \]

Infinitely many solutions, forming a line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

Transpose

Definition

The transpose of \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), written \(\mathbf{A}^T\), swaps rows and columns:

\[ (\mathbf{A}^T)_{ij} = \mathbf{A}_{ji}. \]

Geometrically, it reflects the matrix across its main diagonal.

Properties.

  • \((\mathbf{A}^T)^T = \mathbf{A}\).
  • \((\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{A}^T + \mathbf{B}^T\).
  • \((\mathbf{AB})^T = \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{A}^T\) — note the reversal of order.
  • If \(\mathbf{A}\) is square and \(\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}\), then \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric.
\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{A}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \text{ satisfies } \mathbf{S}^T = \mathbf{S}. \]
Example · verifying \((\mathbf{AB})^T = \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{A}^T\)

Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ 6 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\). First compute the product:

\[ \mathbf{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 6 & 1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 7 \\ 3 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 6 & 3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 19 \\ 24 & 43 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad (\mathbf{AB})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 24 \\ 19 & 43 \end{bmatrix} \]

Now the other side, with \(\mathbf{B}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 6 \\ 5 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{A}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{A}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 2 & 0 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 4 \\ 5 \cdot 1 + 7 \cdot 2 & 5 \cdot 3 + 7 \cdot 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 24 \\ 19 & 43 \end{bmatrix} \quad\checkmark \]

Had we written \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{B}^T\) instead we would have got \(\begin{bmatrix} 18 & 26 \\ 20 & 38\end{bmatrix}\) — which is why the order reversal is not optional.

Matrix inverse

Definition

A square matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is invertible if there exists \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) with

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}_n. \]

Condition: \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible if and only if \(\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0\), equivalently \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\).

The determinant, briefly

For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}\), the determinant is \(\det(\mathbf{A}) = ad - bc\). Geometrically it is the signed area scale factor of the transformation: the unit square of area 1 is mapped to a parallelogram of area \(|\det(\mathbf{A})|\), and a negative sign means orientation was flipped.

A determinant of zero means area was crushed to nothing — information was destroyed, so the map cannot be undone. That is exactly why \(\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0\) is the invertibility condition. The playground below lets you watch this happen.

Further facts.

  • \((\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{-1} = \mathbf{A}\).
  • \((\mathbf{AB})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) — the order reverses, as with the transpose.
  • \((\mathbf{A}^T)^{-1} = (\mathbf{A}^{-1})^T\).

What about rectangular matrices?

Only square matrices have a standard inverse, and even then only when they are full rank (see rank). Rectangular matrices still have useful structure: we can compute their rank, and define a pseudoinverse (the Moore–Penrose inverse) that gives the least-squares solution when an exact one does not exist.

Rank of a matrix

Definition

The rank of \(\mathbf{A}\), written \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\), is the dimension of its column space — equivalently, of its row space. It counts the number of linearly independent columns (or rows), which are always the same number.

Rank–nullity theorem. For \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\): \[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) + \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) = n. \]

Full rank. The matrix

\[ \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}) = 3, \]

since \(\det(\mathbf{B}) = -17 \neq 0\).

Low rank. By contrast,

\[ \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 1 & 1.5 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 & 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{L}) = 1. \]

Every row is a scalar multiple of the first: \(R_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}R_1\), \(R_3 = 2R_1\), \(R_4 = R_1\), \(R_5 = 2R_1\). Five rows, but only one independent direction.

Low rank: curse or blessing?

A low-rank square matrix is singular. Its determinant is zero, it has no inverse, and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) may have no solution or infinitely many. In theory and in practice, that is a problem.

But being low-rank also means the matrix can be described with far fewer numbers. Suppose \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times 1000}\) has rank 5. Then all 1000 rows live in a 5-dimensional subspace, and there exist \(\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times 5}\) and \(\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{5 \times 1000}\) with \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\).

Why this is good

  1. Storage. Instead of \(10^6\) parameters, store \(1000\times5 + 5\times1000 = 10{,}000\) — a hundredfold compression.
  2. Speed. \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\) becomes \(\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x})\), two small products instead of one large one.
  3. Noise reduction. In images and sensor signals the high-rank components often carry the noise. Truncating keeps the structure and drops the rest.
  4. Generalisation. Reducing the rank of weight matrices acts as an implicit regulariser, limiting capacity and discouraging overfitting.
  5. Latent structure. Low-rank decompositions such as SVD and PCA are how hidden low-dimensional structure gets discovered in the first place.

Why it can be bad

  1. Lost expressivity. A low-rank map cannot represent complex relationships; constraining rank constrains capacity, which can underfit rich data.
  2. Information loss. Discarding small singular components can discard subtle but real patterns, especially in noisy or fine-grained data.
  3. Harder optimisation. Rank constraints make the parameter space non-convex and awkward for gradient descent.
  4. Over-simplification. Blindly imposing low rank on transformer or CNN weights degrades performance when higher-rank features carry task-specific information.
Key insight

Low-rankness means redundancy. Exploiting that redundancy buys efficient storage, faster inference, and often better generalisation under resource constraints — which is why it turns up in image compression, low-rank approximation of transformer weights, and model pruning for edge devices.

Python 
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [2, 4, 6], [3, 6, 9]])
np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)     # 1  (every row is a multiple of the first)

B = np.array([[1, 0, 2], [0, 1, 3], [4, 5, 6]])
np.linalg.matrix_rank(B)     # 3  (full rank)

Null space

Definition

The null space (or kernel) of \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) is

\[ \operatorname{Null}(\mathbf{A}) = \{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \,\}. \]

Its dimension is the nullity of \(\mathbf{A}\). It contains every input that \(\mathbf{A}\) sends to the zero vector, and so records exactly the linear dependencies among the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\).

A null space is never empty

\(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\) always holds, so \(\mathbf{0}\) belongs to every null space. The right question is not whether it is empty but how large it is. When only the zero vector is present we call the null space trivial and write \(\{\mathbf{0}\}\) — a set containing exactly one element, which is not the same thing as the empty set \(\varnothing\).

Trivial null space.

\[ \mathbf{I}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{Null}(\mathbf{I}_2) = \{\mathbf{0}\}, \quad \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{I}_2) = 0. \]

Non-trivial null space.

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) = 3 - 1 = 2. \]

A basis for the null space is

\[ \operatorname{Null}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\; \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \]

Check the first: \(\mathbf{A}(1,0,1)^T = (1 + 0 - 1,\; 2 + 0 - 2)^T = \mathbf{0}\) ✓, and the second: \(\mathbf{A}(0,1,2)^T = (0 + 2 - 2,\; 0 + 4 - 4)^T = \mathbf{0}\) ✓. Both are annihilated and they are linearly independent, so together they span the whole 2-dimensional null space.

Common types of matrices

Recognising structure is how large problems become tractable. Each family below buys you something concrete — a cheaper solve, a free inverse, or a guarantee about the eigenvalues.

The matrix zoo

Identity matrix

Ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere. For any \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{I}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\) with matching dimensions.

\[ \mathbf{I}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: the multiplicative identity of matrix algebra; represents "no change" in a linear transformation, and appears in regularisation terms such as \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\).

Diagonal matrix

All off-diagonal entries are zero, so it scales each axis independently.

\[ \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: trivial to invert (reciprocate the diagonal) and to multiply. Appears in eigendecomposition and in Ridge regression penalties.

Symmetric matrix

\(\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}\) — a mirror image across the diagonal.

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: covariance matrices and Hessians are always symmetric. Real symmetric matrices are guaranteed real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis — a very strong guarantee.

Orthogonal matrix

A square matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) whose columns are orthonormal, so that

\[ \mathbf{Q}^T \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^T = \mathbf{I} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{Q}^{-1} = \mathbf{Q}^T. \] \[ \mathbf{Q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: preserves lengths and angles — rotations and reflections. Appears in QR decomposition, in PCA, and in neural network weight initialisation. The inverse being free is the main practical attraction.

"Orthogonal" versus "orthonormal"

These words are easy to trip over. A set of vectors is orthogonal if they are mutually perpendicular, and orthonormal if they are additionally each of unit length. A matrix whose columns are merely orthogonal satisfies \(\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{D}\) for some diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\); orthonormal columns are what give \(\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}\).

Confusingly, the standard term orthogonal matrix refers to the stronger condition: a square matrix with orthonormal columns. There is no separate standard object called an "orthonormal matrix". And for a non-square \(\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}\) with \(k < m\) and orthonormal columns, \(\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_k\) still holds but \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^T \neq \mathbf{I}_m\) — that product is a projection, not the identity.

Example · verifying orthonormal columns
\[ \mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ 0 & -\frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix} \]

Write \(\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \mathbf{q}_3\) for the columns. First, each has unit length:

\[ \|\mathbf{q}_1\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = 1, \qquad \|\mathbf{q}_2\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{4}{6} = 1, \qquad \|\mathbf{q}_3\|^2 = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = 1. \]

Second, they are mutually orthogonal:

\[ \mathbf{q}_1 \cdot \mathbf{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{12}} - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{12}} + 0 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{q}_1 \cdot \mathbf{q}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} + 0 = 0, \] \[ \mathbf{q}_2 \cdot \mathbf{q}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{18}} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{18}} - \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{18}} = 0. \]

Those two facts are the statement \(\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_3\): entry \((i,j)\) of \(\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q}\) is precisely \(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_j\), which is 1 on the diagonal and 0 off it. Written out — note that \(\mathbf{Q}^T\) is the left factor:

\[ \mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & -\frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ 0 & -\frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}_3 . \]

Since \(\mathbf{Q}\) is square, it is an orthogonal matrix and \(\mathbf{Q}^{-1} = \mathbf{Q}^T\).

Skew-symmetric matrix

\(\mathbf{A}^T = -\mathbf{A}\). The diagonal is therefore forced to be zero.

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -1 \\ -2 & 0 & -4 \\ 1 & 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 \\ -1 & -4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = -\mathbf{A}. \quad\checkmark \]

Use: models rotations and angular momentum in physics. Eigenvalues are purely imaginary or zero.

Upper and lower triangular matrices

\[ \mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 \\ 7 & 2 & 0 \\ 5 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: essential to LU decomposition. Triangular systems solve by forward or backward substitution in \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) instead of requiring a full \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) inversion, and the determinant is just the product of the diagonal.

Sparse matrix

Most entries are zero.

\[ \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: ubiquitous in recommender systems, NLP bag-of-words features, and graph adjacency matrices. Storing only the non-zeros enables memory-efficient storage and lets the arithmetic skip most of the work.

Rank-one matrix

Expressible as an outer product of two vectors, \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T\).

\[ \mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

Use: the atom of low-rank approximation — every SVD writes a matrix as a weighted sum of rank-one pieces.

Vector and matrix norms

Definition

Vector norms.

\[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|, \qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i x_i^2}, \qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|. \]

Matrix norm (Frobenius).

\[ \|\mathbf{A}\|_F = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2}. \]

This is just the \(\ell_2\) norm of the matrix flattened into one long vector.

Norm axioms. Anything calling itself a norm must satisfy:

  • Non-negativity and definiteness: \(\|\mathbf{x}\| \ge 0\), with \(\|\mathbf{x}\| = 0 \iff \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Absolute homogeneity: \(\|\alpha\mathbf{x}\| = |\alpha|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\).
  • Triangle inequality: \(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\| \le \|\mathbf{x}\| + \|\mathbf{y}\|\).

What "length 1" looks like

Slide \(p\) from 1 towards \(\infty\) and watch the unit ball morph from a diamond to a circle to a square. Those corners at \(p=1\) are why LASSO produces exact zeros.

Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and diagonalisation

Definition

Let \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\). A nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(\mathbf{A}\) if

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \]

for some scalar \(\lambda\), the associated eigenvalue. For a real matrix \(\lambda\) may be real or complex; real symmetric matrices are guaranteed real eigenvalues.

Geometric intuition

Most vectors change both magnitude and direction when hit with \(\mathbf{A}\). Eigenvectors are the rare directions that do not rotate — they are only scaled, stretched or compressed (and possibly flipped). That makes them the natural "axes" of the transformation, and the eigenvalue is how much stretching happens along each one.

Hunt for an eigenvector

Rotate \(\mathbf{v}\) until \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\) lines up with it. When the arrows are parallel you have found one — and the length ratio is \(\lambda\). Try a rotation matrix to see a case with no real eigenvector at all.

Play

Diagonalisation

If \(\mathbf{A}\) is diagonalisable, then

\[ \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}, \]

where \(\mathbf{V}\) has the eigenvectors as columns and \(\mathbf{\Lambda}\) is diagonal with the corresponding eigenvalues. This makes powers almost free:

\[ \mathbf{A}^k = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}^k\mathbf{V}^{-1}. \]

Computing \(\mathbf{A}^{100}\mathbf{x}\) becomes \(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{100}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\) — raise \(n\) numbers to the hundredth power instead of performing a hundred matrix multiplications. Cheaper, and numerically more stable.

  • If \(\mathbf{A}\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors, it is diagonalisable.
  • Real symmetric matrices are always diagonalisable, and with an orthonormal eigenbasis — so \(\mathbf{V}\) can be taken orthogonal and \(\mathbf{V}^{-1} = \mathbf{V}^T\).

Why this matters

In machine learning: eigenvalues and eigenvectors identify the most informative directions in data (PCA), reveal how the loss surface curves and hence how optimisation will behave, describe the structure of graphs in graph-based models, and indicate whether an attention mechanism is focusing too narrowly or too broadly.

In engineering more broadly: the eigenvalues of a system's state matrix determine stability in control systems; impedance and admittance matrices reveal resonances in circuit design; and the singular values used for image compression are built from the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\) — see SVD.

Examples

Diagonal. For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal: \(\lambda_1 = 2\), \(\lambda_2 = 3\), with eigenvectors \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\).

Non-diagonal. For \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), solve the characteristic equation:

\[ \det(\mathbf{B} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) = 0 \]

giving \(\lambda_1 = 5\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2\). This is the matrix loaded in the playground above.

Singular value decomposition

Definition

For any matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) there exist orthogonal matrices \(\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) and \(\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), and a rectangular diagonal \(\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) with non-negative entries \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0\) (the singular values), such that

\[ \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^T. \]

Here \(r = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\). Unlike eigendecomposition this works for every real matrix — square or not, invertible or not, diagonalisable or not.

Existence yes, uniqueness no

The SVD always exists, and the singular values are unique. But \(\mathbf{U}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) are not: flipping the sign of a column \(\mathbf{u}_i\) together with the matching \(\mathbf{v}_i\) leaves \(\mathbf{A}\) unchanged, and when a singular value is repeated you may rotate freely within its subspace. The decomposition is therefore unique only up to those choices — which is why two libraries can hand you different-looking but equally valid factors for the same matrix.

What it means geometrically

SVD expresses any linear transformation as a sequence of three operations:

  1. Rotate (or reflect) the input space via \(\mathbf{V}^T\),
  2. Stretch or shrink along the coordinate axes using \(\mathbf{S}\),
  3. Rotate again via \(\mathbf{U}\).

Rotation → scaling → rotation. That is the whole story, and it is why the image of the unit circle is always an ellipse.

Circle in, ellipse out

Scrub through the three stages one at a time. The semi-axes of the final ellipse are exactly \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\).

Play

Key properties

  • \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{S}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^T\) and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^T\mathbf{U}^T\).
  • The columns of \(\mathbf{U}\) are eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\); the columns of \(\mathbf{V}\) are eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\).
  • \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\) equals the number of non-zero singular values.
  • The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\) — the precise link back to eigendecomposition.
  • Truncating to the largest \(k\) singular values gives the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation in Frobenius norm (the Eckart–Young theorem).
Example

For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\), compute \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are 1 and 4. Taking square roots and ordering:

\[ \sigma_1 = 2, \qquad \sigma_2 = 1. \]

Singular values are always non-negative, even though this matrix has a negative entry — the sign is absorbed into \(\mathbf{U}\).

Summary intuition

SVD takes a complicated transformation and peels it into orthogonal building blocks. It answers three questions at once: which directions does this matrix care about most, how much does it stretch each one, and what can I safely throw away? That is why the same decomposition underlies image compression, denoising, PCA, and low-rank compression of network weights.

Python 
A = np.array([[1., 0.], [0., -2.]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)        # s = [2., 1.]

# Rank-k approximation of any matrix
def rank_k(A, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    return (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

# Fraction of "energy" retained by the first k components
energy = np.cumsum(s**2) / np.sum(s**2)

Sample space and random variables

Definition

The sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.

  • Tossing two coins: \(\Omega = \{\mathrm{HH}, \mathrm{HT}, \mathrm{TH}, \mathrm{TT}\}\).
  • Measuring a temperature: \(\Omega = \mathbb{R}\).

A random variable \(X\) is a function \(X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) assigning a number to each outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\). This is what lets us do arithmetic instead of bookkeeping over outcomes.

Notation. Capitals \(X, Y, Z\) for random variables; lowercase \(x, y, z\) for particular realisations. So \(\mathbb{P}(X = x)\) reads "the probability that the random variable \(X\) takes the specific value \(x\)".

Discrete random variables

A discrete random variable \(X\) takes values in a countable set \(\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}\).

Definition

Probability mass function (pmf).

\[ p_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x), \quad x \in \mathcal{X}, \qquad \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p_X(x) = 1. \]

Cumulative distribution function (cdf).

\[ F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x) = \sum_{t \le x} p_X(t). \]
Example · number of heads in two flips

With \(X \in \{0,1,2\}\) counting heads in two fair flips:

\[ p_X(0) = \tfrac{1}{4}, \qquad p_X(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad p_X(2) = \tfrac{1}{4}. \]

There are four equally likely outcomes; one gives zero heads, two give exactly one, and one gives two. The probabilities sum to 1, as they must.

Common discrete distributions

  • Bernoulli(\(p\)). A single yes/no trial, success with probability \(p\). \(\mathbb{E}[X] = p\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1-p)\). Uses: a coin flip, a spam indicator.
  • Binomial(\(n, p\)). Number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials: \[ \mathbb{P}(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p). \] Uses: defect counts in manufacturing, heads in repeated flips.
  • Poisson(\(\lambda\)). Counts of rare events in a fixed window: \[ \mathbb{P}(X=k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda. \] Uses: arrivals per hour, typos per page. Mean and variance coincide, which is a useful diagnostic in practice.
  • Multinomial(\(n, \boldsymbol{\pi}\)). Counts across multiple categories — the generalisation of the binomial. Uses: customer choices over \(K\) products.

Moments of a discrete random variable

Definition

Expectation — the probability-weighted average:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p_X(x). \]

Variance — the expected squared deviation from the mean:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2. \]

The second form is usually the easier one to compute, and is worth committing to memory.

Numerical example. If \(X \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p = 0.3)\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.3\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21\).

Joint, marginal and conditional probability

Definition

Joint pmf.

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \mathbb{P}(X=x, Y=y), \qquad \sum_{x,y} p_{X,Y}(x,y) = 1. \]

Marginal pmf — sum away the variable you do not care about:

\[ p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y), \qquad p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Conditional pmf — take a slice and renormalise:

\[ \mathbb{P}(X = x \mid Y = y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}. \]

Joint, marginal, conditional — one table

Switch views to see marginalising as summing a row or column, and conditioning as isolating a slice and rescaling it back to sum to 1.

Example
\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} p_{X,Y}(x,y) & y=0 & y=1 & y=2 \\\hline x=0 & 0.1 & 0.1 & 0.1 \\ x=1 & 0.2 & 0.2 & 0.3 \end{array} \]

Summing the second row gives the marginal \(p_X(1) = 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.7\). Summing the last column gives \(p_Y(2) = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4\). Conditioning on \(Y = 2\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(X = 1 \mid Y = 2) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(1,2)}{p_Y(2)} = \frac{0.3}{0.4} = 0.75. \]

(All six entries sum to 1, so this is a valid joint distribution.)

Covariance and correlation

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}. \]

Covariance carries the units of \(X\) times the units of \(Y\), which makes its magnitude hard to interpret on its own. Correlation divides that scale out, so \(\rho\) always lies in \([-1, 1]\).

Continuous random variables

Definition

A continuous random variable \(X\) has a probability density function \(f_X(x)\) with

\[ f_X(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\, dx = 1. \]

Probabilities are areas under that density, never heights:

\[ \mathbb{P}(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x)\, dx, \qquad \mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0. \]

And the cdf accumulates that area: \(F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\, dt\).

Density is not probability

\(f_X(x)\) can exceed 1 — a Uniform\((0, 0.5)\) has density 2 everywhere on its support. What must not exceed 1 is the area. This is also why \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0\) for every single point: a point has no width, so it encloses no area. Probability lives in intervals.

Common continuous distributions

  • Uniform(\(a, b\)). Constant density \(\frac{1}{b-a}\) on \([a,b]\). The "I know the range and nothing else" model.
  • Normal(\(\mu, \sigma^2\)). The bell curve. Models measurement noise, heights, aggregated small effects. The 68–95–99.7 rule says roughly 68% of the mass lies within \(\mu \pm \sigma\), 95% within \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\), and 99.7% within \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\).
  • Exponential(\(\lambda\)). Waiting time between Poisson events. It is memoryless: \(\mathbb{P}(X > s+t \mid X > s) = \mathbb{P}(X > t)\) — having waited already tells you nothing about how much longer you must wait.
  • Multivariate normal. Generalises the normal to \(d\) dimensions, parameterised by a mean vector and a covariance matrix.

Expectation and variance replace the sums with integrals:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, f_X(x)\, dx, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \int (x - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 f_X(x)\, dx. \]

Law of total probability and Bayes' theorem

Definition

Law of total probability. If \(\{B_i\}\) partitions the sample space with \(\mathbb{P}(B_i) > 0\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_i \mathbb{P}(A \mid B_i)\, \mathbb{P}(B_i). \]

Bayes' theorem. For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B) > 0\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\, \mathbb{P}(A)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}. \]

In densities: \( f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \dfrac{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)\, f_X(x)}{f_Y(y)} \).

The base-rate trap, in 1000 people

A 95%-accurate test for a 1%-prevalence disease. Read the posterior off the dots, then try to talk yourself out of it.

Play
Example · a medical test

Suppose a disease has prevalence \(\mathbb{P}(D) = 0.01\), the test has sensitivity \(\mathbb{P}(+ \mid D) = 0.95\), and a false-positive rate \(\mathbb{P}(+ \mid \overline{D}) = 0.05\). By the law of total probability:

\[ \mathbb{P}(+) = 0.95(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059. \]

Then Bayes' theorem gives the posterior:

\[ \mathbb{P}(D \mid +) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.059} = \frac{0.0095}{0.059} \approx 0.161. \]

A positive result on a 95%-accurate test still leaves only a 16% chance of having the disease. The reason is arithmetic, not paradox: 99% of people are healthy, so their 5% false positive rate produces about 49.5 false alarms per 1000 — far more than the 9.5 genuine detections.

Independence and conditional independence

Definition

Independence. \(X \perp Y\) exactly when the joint factorises:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y) \quad \text{(continuous)} \]

for all \((x,y)\).

Conditional independence. \(X \perp Y \mid Z\) when

\[ p_{X,Y \mid Z}(x,y \mid z) = p_{X \mid Z}(x \mid z)\, p_{Y \mid Z}(y \mid z) \]

for every \(z\) of positive probability or density.

Numerical example. Let \(X, Y\) be independent fair coin flips. Then \(p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \tfrac14\) for all \(x, y \in \{0,1\}\), and indeed \(p_X(x)\,p_Y(y) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14\). ✓

Why conditional independence earns its keep

Conditional independence is the assumption that makes large probabilistic models tractable. It is exactly what the "naïve" in Naïve Bayes refers to: features are assumed independent given the class label, which turns an impossible joint distribution into a product of small, estimable ones. The assumption is usually false and the classifier often works anyway.

Sample mean and sample variance

Definition

Given i.i.d. samples \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) from a distribution with finite mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \overline{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \overline{X}_n)^2. \]

The \(n-1\) in the denominator (rather than \(n\)) is what makes \(S_n^2\) an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\): one degree of freedom was already spent estimating the mean.

Properties.

  • \(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_n] = \mu\) — the sample mean is unbiased.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline{X}_n) = \sigma^2 / n\) — averaging shrinks variance, and the standard error therefore falls like \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
Example

For the sample \((3, 5, 2, 4)\) with \(n = 4\):

\[ \overline{X}_4 = \frac{3+5+2+4}{4} = 3.5, \] \[ S_4^2 = \frac{(3-3.5)^2 + (5-3.5)^2 + (2-3.5)^2 + (4-3.5)^2}{3} = \frac{0.25 + 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25}{3} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.6667. \]

Part 1.3 · Calculus & Optimisation

Training a model means minimising a loss, and minimising means following derivatives. This part covers the calculus that makes that possible — with both the symbolic rules and the geometric picture.

Limits and derivatives

Definition

Limit. We write \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) if \(f(x)\) gets arbitrarily close to \(L\) as \(x\) gets close to \(a\).

Derivative. The derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) is

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \]

the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) at \(a\) — equivalently, the slope of the tangent line.

Rules.

  • Power rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n x^{n-1}\).
  • Sum rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)+g(x)] = f'(x) + g'(x)\).
  • Product rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)\).

  • Chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\).

Partial derivatives and gradients

Definition

Partial derivative. For \(f(x,y)\):

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h, y) - f(x,y)}{h}, \]

measuring how \(f\) changes with \(x\) while \(y\) is held fixed.

Gradient. For \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\), collect them into a vector:

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}^T . \]

It points in the direction of steepest ascent, which is why descent moves against it.

Example. If \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\) then \(\nabla f(x,y) = (2x, 2y)^T\), so at \((1,2)\) the gradient is \((2,4)^T\).

Vector and matrix derivatives

In machine learning we optimise scalar-valued functions \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) that depend on vectors or matrices — loss functions, in other words. Being able to differentiate with respect to a whole vector or matrix is what makes gradient-based training possible.

A word on layout conventions

Matrix calculus has two competing conventions, and mixing them is a classic source of transpose bugs:

  • Denominator layout (also called gradient layout): \(\partial y / \partial \mathbf{x}\) has the same shape as \(\mathbf{x}\). For scalar \(y\) and column vector \(\mathbf{x}\), the result is a column vector.
  • Numerator layout (Jacobian layout): the result follows the shape of the numerator, so the same derivative comes out as a row vector — the transpose of the above.

These notes use denominator layout throughout, so \(\nabla f\) always has the same shape as the thing you are differentiating with respect to. That is the convention deep learning frameworks follow, and it is what makes the update \(\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla f\) type-check without a transpose.

Scalar function of a vector. For \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\), the gradient is the column vector of partials:

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Two that come up constantly:

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{a}, \qquad f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = 2\mathbf{x}. \]

Scalar function of a matrix. For \(f: \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathbb{R}\), the derivative is an \(m \times n\) matrix with

\[ \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{X}}\right]_{ij} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{X}_{ij}}. \]

For instance, \(f(\mathbf{X}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{X}) \Rightarrow \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{A}\).

The trace, briefly

The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries, \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = \sum_i a_{ii}\). It is linear, satisfies the cyclic property \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{AB}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{BA})\), and equals the sum of the eigenvalues. The Frobenius inner product is \(\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{X}\rangle_F = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij}x_{ij} = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{X})\) — note the transpose, which is easy to drop and matters below.

Vector function of a vector (the Jacobian). For \(\mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\):

\[ J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}. \]

For \(\mathbf{f}(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} x+y \\ x^2 y \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ J_{\mathbf{f}}(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2xy & x^2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Second derivatives (the Hessian). For \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\):

\[ H_f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \mathbf{x}^2} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2} \end{bmatrix}. \]

For \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^2\):

\[ H_f = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Hessian is symmetric because mixed partials commute under mild smoothness conditions (Clairaut's theorem) — which in turn guarantees it has real eigenvalues, the fact that makes the second-derivative test work at all.

The chain rule for multivariable functions

For \(z = f(y)\) and \(y = g(x)\), so that \(z = f(g(x))\):

\[ \frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{df}{dy} \cdot \frac{dg}{dx}. \]

In differential form the reason is almost visible:

\[ dz = \frac{df}{dy}\, dy, \qquad dy = \frac{dg}{dx}\, dx \;\Longrightarrow\; dz = \frac{df}{dy}\cdot\frac{dg}{dx}\, dx, \]

and dividing through by \(dx\) recovers the rule. For the multivariable case with \(\mathbf{u} = g(\mathbf{x})\), the Jacobian takes over:

\[ \nabla_\mathbf{x} f(g(\mathbf{x})) = J_g(\mathbf{x})^T\, \nabla f(g(\mathbf{x})). \]

Matrix calculus identities

Four identities do most of the work in practice:

  1. \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = \mathbf{a}\).
  2. \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) with \(\mathbf{A}\) symmetric \(\Rightarrow \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = 2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\).
  3. \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = 2\mathbf{x}\).
  4. \(f(\mathbf{X}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}) \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{X} f = \mathbf{A}^T\).
Derivations

1 · Linear form. Let \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{x}\) with \(\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) constant.

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i x_i \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = a_i \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = \mathbf{a}. \]

2 · Quadratic form. Let \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) with \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) symmetric.

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n x_i A_{ij} x_j \]

Differentiating with respect to \(x_k\) picks up the terms where \(k\) appears in either index:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k} = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{kj}x_j + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i A_{ik} = [\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}]_k + [\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{x}]_k. \]

Since \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^T\), the two terms coincide:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k} = 2[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}]_k \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = 2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}. \]

Without symmetry the general result is \(\nabla_\mathbf{x} f = (\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^T)\mathbf{x}\).

3 · Squared norm. Let \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}\).

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = 2x_i \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = 2\mathbf{x}. \]

This is just identity 2 with \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\).

4 · Trace. Let \(f(\mathbf{X}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X})\). For the product to be square we need \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) and \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\). Expanding the trace:

\[ \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^m [\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}]_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} X_{ji}. \]

The entry \(X_{kl}\) appears exactly once, multiplied by \(A_{lk}\), so

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{kl}} = A_{lk} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla_\mathbf{X} f = \mathbf{A}^T. \]

Watch the index order carefully: it is \(A_{ij}X_{ji}\), not \(A_{ij}X_{ij}\). The latter sum is the Frobenius inner product \(\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X}\rangle_F = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{X})\), whose derivative is \(\mathbf{A}\) rather than \(\mathbf{A}^T\). The two identities differ by exactly one transpose, and that transpose is where the sign errors live.

Example (identity 1). For a linear model \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b\) with scalar bias \(b\), the gradient with respect to the input is \(\nabla_\mathbf{x} f = \mathbf{w}\), and with respect to the weights it is \(\nabla_\mathbf{w} f = \mathbf{x}\).

Convexity and optimisation

Definition

A set \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) is convex if for any \(x, y \in C\) the whole segment \(\theta x + (1-\theta)y\), \(0 \le \theta \le 1\), stays inside \(C\).

A function \(f\) is convex if its domain is convex and

\[ f(\theta x + (1-\theta)y) \le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y). \]

First-order condition. For differentiable \(f\), convexity is equivalent to

\[ f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T (y - x) \quad \text{for all } x, y, \]

i.e. the tangent plane is a global underestimator.

Why anyone cares about convexity

For a convex function, every local minimum is a global minimum. There are no false bottoms to get stuck in, so gradient descent that stops moving has genuinely finished. Neural network losses are famously not convex — which is exactly why initialisation, learning-rate schedules and momentum matter so much in practice.

Optimisation basics

  • A critical point satisfies \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}\).
  • The Hessian classifies it: at a critical point, \(H \succ 0\) (all eigenvalues positive) gives a local minimum, \(H \prec 0\) gives a local maximum, and mixed signs give a saddle point.
  • The gradient descent update is \(\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{x}^{(k)} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\), where \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

Example. For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(f'(x) = 2x\), so the only critical point is \(x = 0\). Since \(f''(0) = 2 > 0\) it is a minimum — and because \(f\) is convex, it is the global one.

Inner products, orthogonality and orthonormal bases

Definition

Inner product. For \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n\):

\[ \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}. \]

Orthogonality. \(\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b}\) when \(\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\rangle = 0\).

Orthonormal set. \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) is orthonormal if \(\langle \mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}\) — mutually orthogonal and each of unit length. If it also spans a subspace, it is an orthonormal basis of that subspace.

Two inequalities worth knowing by heart.

\[ |\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle| \le \|\mathbf{a}\|_2\,\|\mathbf{b}\|_2 \quad \text{(Cauchy–Schwarz)}, \qquad \|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\|_2 \le \|\mathbf{a}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{b}\|_2 \quad \text{(triangle)}. \]

Cauchy–Schwarz is what makes the cosine similarity \(\cos\theta = \frac{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|}\) well-defined — it guarantees the ratio never leaves \([-1,1]\), so it really is a cosine.

Examples. The standard basis is orthonormal:

\[ \mathbf{e}_1 = [1,0]^T,\ \mathbf{e}_2 = [0,1]^T \;\Rightarrow\; \langle \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\rangle = 0, \quad \|\mathbf{e}_i\| = 1. \]

And a rotated pair is too:

\[ \mathbf{u}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1]^T, \quad \mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[-1,1]^T, \quad \mathbf{u}_1^T\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(-1 + 1) = 0. \]

Positive (semi)definite matrices

Definition

A symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is

  • positive definite (PD) if \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} > 0\) for all \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\),
  • positive semidefinite (PSD) if \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \ge 0\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\).

These are the matrix analogues of "positive number" and "non-negative number", and they behave that way in almost every respect that matters.

Equivalent characterisations (for symmetric \(\mathbf{A}\)):

  • PD \(\iff\) all eigenvalues \(> 0\); PSD \(\iff\) all eigenvalues \(\ge 0\).
  • PD \(\iff\) there exists an invertible \(\mathbf{R}\) with \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R}\) (the Cholesky factorisation).

Examples.

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow \lambda = 1, 3 \ (\text{both} > 0) \Rightarrow \text{PD}, \] \[ \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow \lambda = 0, 2 \ (\text{both} \ge 0) \Rightarrow \text{PSD but not PD}. \]

For \(\mathbf{B}\), the vector \(\mathbf{x} = (1,1)^T\) gives \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x} = 0\) despite \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\) — the zero eigenvalue in action.

Where these show up

Every covariance matrix is PSD (a variance cannot be negative). Every kernel matrix must be PSD for the kernel trick to be valid. And a Hessian that is PD at a critical point certifies a local minimum. Recognising definiteness is often the shortest route to knowing whether a method will work.

Python 
A = np.array([[2, -1], [-1, 2]])
vals = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)     # eigvalsh: for symmetric matrices
is_pd  = np.all(vals > 0)        # True
is_psd = np.all(vals >= 0)       # True

Quadratic forms

Definition

For \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) and \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), the scalar

\[ Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \]

is a quadratic form.

Notes.

  • If \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\min} \le \cdots \le \lambda_{\max}\), then \(Q\) is trapped between them: \[ \lambda_{\min}\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \le \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \le \lambda_{\max}\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2. \]
  • If \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\), then \(Q(\mathbf{x}) = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2\).

Orthogonal projections

Definition

Onto a vector. The projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto a nonzero \(\mathbf{a}\) is

\[ \operatorname{proj}_\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2}\,\mathbf{a}, \]

the closest vector to \(\mathbf{b}\) in \(\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}\}\).

Onto a subspace. If \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}\) has full column rank and its columns form a basis for \(\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m\), the orthogonal projection matrix onto \(\mathcal{S}\) is

\[ \mathbf{P}_\mathcal{S} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T, \qquad \text{with } \mathbf{P}_\mathcal{S}^2 = \mathbf{P}_\mathcal{S} = \mathbf{P}_\mathcal{S}^T. \]

If \(\mathbf{Q}\) has orthonormal columns spanning \(\mathcal{S}\), this simplifies to \(\mathbf{P}_\mathcal{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^T\).

Example · onto a 2-D subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

First \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\), so \((\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\), giving

\[ \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Applying it to \(\mathbf{b}\):

\[ \mathbf{P}\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} (2)(2) + (1)(0) + (-1)(1) \\ (1)(2) + (2)(0) + (1)(1) \\ (-1)(2) + (1)(0) + (2)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Two checks that this is right. First, \((1,1,0)^T\) is the first column of \(\mathbf{A}\), so it genuinely lies in the subspace. Second, the residual \(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{P}\mathbf{b} = (1,-1,1)^T\) is orthogonal to both columns: \((1,-1,1)\cdot(1,1,0) = 0\) ✓ and \((1,-1,1)\cdot(0,1,1) = 0\) ✓.

This is linear regression

Least squares asks for the \(\mathbf{x}\) minimising \(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2\). Since \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) can only ever reach the column space of \(\mathbf{A}\), the best possible answer is the point in that space closest to \(\mathbf{b}\) — its projection. The normal equations \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{b}\) are nothing more than the statement that the residual must be orthogonal to every column of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Law of large numbers and central limit theorem

Definition

(Weak) law of large numbers. Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Then

\[ \overline{X}_n \xrightarrow{\ \mathbb{P}\ } \mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty, \]

meaning \(\mathbb{P}(|\overline{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Central limit theorem. Under the same conditions,

\[ \sqrt{n}\,\frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{D}\ } \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. \]

The standardised sample mean converges in distribution to a standard normal.

Interpretation. The LLN says the sample mean settles near the true mean as \(n\) grows. The CLT says more: after centring and scaling, the distribution of that sample mean is approximately normal for large \(n\), regardless of what the parent distribution looked like (under mild conditions). Together they explain why averages are so well behaved, and why normality assumptions are so often defensible in practice.

Key idea

Big-\(\mathcal{O}\) tells you how cost responds to size. Doubling the matrix size multiplies addition by 4 and multiplication by 8:

\[ n \to 2n: \quad \mathcal{O}(n^2) \to 4\times, \qquad \mathcal{O}(n^3) \to 8\times. \]

The computational bottleneck in deep learning is matrix multiplication. That single fact is why so much effort goes into shrinking the footprint of matrices — reducing rank, increasing sparsity, quantising weights — since every one of those directly attacks the \(mnk\).

Further reading

Recommended for going deeper on anything in this chapter:

  • The Matrix Cookbook — Kaare Brandt Petersen and Michael Syskind Pedersen. A reference, not a textbook: the identity you need is in it.
